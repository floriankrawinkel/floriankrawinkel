PAULA

Mixed Reality · Unity · Meta Quest 3 · C# · Meta SDK · Hand Tracking · Physical Prototyping · User Testing

P.A.U.L.A. — short for Pump-Action-User-Learning-Application — is a Mixed Reality training simulation developed in collaboration with the Cologne Fire Department. The project was created as a three-person student project. I worked together with two other students to explore how Mixed Reality could be used to simulate the operation of a fire pump without requiring access to an actual firefighting vehicle during every training session.

The project focused on a specific question:

How does integrating a physical object into an Mixed Reality training simulation influence immersion compared to purely virtual training methods?

Rather than creating a completely virtual training experience, the project combined a physical replica of a fire pump with a digitally reconstructed version of the same object. The goal was to investigate whether interacting with something physically present could make the virtual experience feel more tangible and realistic. For me, the project was particularly interesting because it required working across several areas at once: 3D modeling, Mixed Reality development, physical prototyping, interaction design, user testing, and research. It also showed me that designing an MR experience is not simply about creating a convincing virtual environment. The relationship between the physical and digital layers becomes equally important. Training firefighters to operate technical equipment such as a fire pump normally requires access to the actual machinery installed on a firefighting vehicle. This creates practical limitations. The equipment is expensive, needs to remain available for operational purposes, and cannot simply be used as a training object whenever needed.

The project therefore explored whether an MR simulation could recreate the essential training process using a much more accessible physical setup. The challenge was not only to reproduce the appearance of the fire pump. The simulation needed to communicate the correct sequence of actions and allow users to interact with the relevant controls.

At the same time, the project presented an interesting interaction-design question. A fully virtual simulation gives the user complete freedom over the interface and interaction design, but it can lack the physical feedback of handling real equipment. A physical replica, on the other hand, can provide tangible feedback, but only if the physical and virtual objects correspond closely enough.

This led to the central research question and ultimately shaped many of the design decisions throughout the project.
